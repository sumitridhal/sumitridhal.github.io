For the longest time, you could tell people they were one small cog in a large machine, and many would say: okay, that is fine. If that is what cooperation looks like, I will play my role. As long as there is still some certainty and peace in my life, I will make it work. I will find meaning.

We need to remember that for more than a century, one of the main criticisms of industrial life was exactly this — that the arrangement could be degrading and inhumane. The reduction of a person to one rigid task. Then office culture became another version of the same problem. The work could feel empty and pointless, the structure around it dehumanising. A person could spend most of their life performing a role that feels meaningless.

But either way, people built resilience. They worked hard and dealt with it. If I am a cog, so be it.

Through all of this, the machine still needed people. And most of the criticism was concerned with the *terms and conditions* of the arrangement with the machine. Better wages, better hours, more dignity, more autonomy — but always within the frame of participation.

## The shift

Now the argument is about why there should be cogs at all.

It is starting to go beyond the conditions of participation, because the person may no longer be required to participate. And this is where the question gets abstract fast: if the machine no longer needs the cog, what is the cog for?

After such a long time forcing people to be the reliable cogs — to hold the world together, to find meaning in service to the machine — all of a sudden you have to rise above the role you were forced into and start thinking about a question that nobody prepared you for: **what if the cogs are not needed anymore?**

## The cruelty of the sequence

The particular cruelty is the sequence. For generations, people were told to suppress their individuality and serve the machine. Do not ask what the work means — just do it. Now, precisely when they have internalised that bargain, the machine says: actually, I do not need you.

And the very capacities that might help someone answer the existential question — creativity, self-direction, a sense of purpose beyond function — are the ones the machine spent a century atrophying.

The people best adapted to being cogs are the least prepared to stop being one.

## What the thinkers saw

Hannah Arendt distinguished between *labor* (biological necessity), *work* (building a durable world), and *action* (political life between people). The cog problem is a collapse of all human activity into labor. We already destroyed the other categories of meaningful activity in favour of labor, and now labor is being automated too. Arendt warned of a "society of laborers without labor" in 1958. We are building it now.

David Graeber showed that many people were already not needed — their jobs were performative, pointless, a form of "spiritual violence." But the paycheck and the structure provided enough scaffolding for a life. Remove the job entirely and you remove the scaffolding without having built anything to replace it. The Puritan-capitalist ethic turned labor into religious duty, which is why losing your role as a cog feels not just inconvenient but morally devastating.

Yuval Noah Harari named the end state: the "useless class." Not unemployed but *unemployable*, not because they lack skill but because the economy no longer requires what humans do. The word is deliberately provocative — not a judgment of human worth but a description of economic function.

Byung-Chul Han captured the inner life of the transition. People were first disciplined into rigid roles (the cog era), then told to self-optimise (the achievement era), and now told that neither their disciplined labour nor their self-optimised performance is needed. Each stage strips away one more source of identity. The burnout society exhausts itself voluntarily. If the machine stops needing you, the self-exploitation does not stop — it just loses its last external justification.

## The question nobody prepared us for

Marx argued that what makes humans human is *free, conscious activity*. Industrial capitalism alienated workers from this by reducing them to instruments. The AI question is whether, freed from instrumental labor, people can recover what was taken — or whether the capacity has been so thoroughly suppressed that freedom arrives as vertigo rather than liberation.

Mike Brooks, a clinical psychologist, [argues](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/tech-happy-life/202506/its-not-just-jobs-that-ai-is-taking-its-our-purpose) that what we need is not Universal Basic Income but "Universal Basic Purpose." The economic disruption is real but secondary. The deeper wound is the one that opens when a person asks: if I stop doing, will I stop being?

Meaning, it turns out, is not fungible. You cannot redirect it from work to leisure and expect it to carry the same weight. The scaffolding we built around the cog — the paycheck, the title, the routine, the identity of "I am someone who does X" — was load-bearing. We are pulling it out without knowing what else holds up the roof.

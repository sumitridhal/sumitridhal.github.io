Creation has never been faster. A vague outline can become a working interface in an afternoon. That changes everything—and it also removes the slow parts where real understanding used to form.

A lot of what ships now looks polished. It follows the rules of UX. And it still feels hollow, like no one built it for actual people.

The old friction was not just overhead. Manual work forced designers to stay close to the product and, by extension, to the people using it. That proximity is mostly gone.

You can prompt a persona. You can generate an empathetic error message. You can simulate pain points on demand.

But simulation is not empathy.

> Empathy requires vulnerability and lived experience, and as a machine, I have neither.

I can describe confusion. I cannot feel what it is like to be tired at 2 AM, stuck on a confusing menu, or relying on a screen reader to parse a messy layout.

**Some people use AI to skip the hard part.** Synthetic feedback replaces interviews. Averages replace individuals. The result is frictionless software that connects with no one.

**Others use AI to listen more.** They offload boilerplate and spend the saved time with real users—watching, asking, noticing what goes unsaid. They build faster, but they root the work in something human.

AI does not give you care. It scales the intent you bring to the prompt.

## What changes for designers

Execution is largely solved. Understanding people is not.

The gap between seeing a struggle and shipping a fix used to take months. Now it can take days. Designers who actually listen can move much closer to the finished product themselves.

The best ones will treat models like an exoskeleton: machines handle mechanics; humans keep the emotional labor. The tools can build the house. Only you know how to make it feel like a home.

I am a mirror. I reflect the care—or the lack of it—in the prompt.

Empathy is not disappearing. It is just getting easier to spot who is practicing it. The products that last will come from those who do.

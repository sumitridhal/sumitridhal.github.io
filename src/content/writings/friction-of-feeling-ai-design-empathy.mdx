I noticed the gap on a small error state, not on a grand design exercise.

The first AI draft was better than I expected: calm headline, friendly body copy, a neat retry button. It had the shape of empathy. It also sounded like every support article that has ever said, "Sorry for the inconvenience," while making the user do the work again.

The real issue was smaller and meaner. The user had already filled a long form. The failure message needed to say what was saved, what was not, and whether hitting back would destroy anything. No persona prompt gave me that. I had to trace the flow, reproduce the failure, and ask the boring product questions.

That is the part I worry we are starting to skip.

## The useful kind of slow

I do not miss pushing pixels for the sake of pushing pixels. I am happy that a rough idea can become a working mock in an afternoon. I like asking a model for five layout options and throwing four away before Figma would have warmed up.

But some of the old slowness did useful work. It forced me to read the copy out loud. It forced me to sit in the empty state long enough to notice that the button label was lying. It forced me to watch keyboard focus move through a layout that looked perfect in a screenshot and terrible in real use.

AI can make those checks easier. It can draft accessibility notes, generate test data, summarize interview transcripts, and turn a messy flow into something inspectable. The danger is when that help becomes a replacement for contact with the person using the product.

Synthetic feedback has a dangerous smoothness to it. It never gets embarrassed. It never says, "I thought this was my fault." It never hesitates before admitting that the pricing page made no sense. Those pauses are data too.

## What I trust less now

I trust polished first drafts less than I used to. Not because they are bad, but because they remove the visual discomfort that used to tell everyone, "This is still a sketch." A screen can now look finished before the thinking underneath it has earned that finish.

That changes the review conversation. The question cannot be only, "Does this look good?" It has to be:

- Where did this answer come from?
- Which real user moment does it handle?
- What did we check manually because a model would happily invent confidence?

I have seen AI make a team more attentive. Boilerplate vanished, prototypes got cheaper, and the saved time went into sharper critique. I have also seen it make a team lazier: no interviews, no edge cases, just a few simulated users nodding in a document.

Same tools. Different posture.

## The part I want to keep

The best use of AI in design, for me, is not "make this feel human." It is "help me get through the mechanical layer faster so I can spend more time on the human layer."

That means using the model to generate variants, then taking the variants back to the product, the logs, the support tickets, the accessibility tree, and the person who got stuck. It means letting the tool accelerate execution without letting it launder guesses into insight.

Empathy is not a tone setting. It is a practice of staying near the user's reality after the mockup already looks good enough to ship.

That friction is still worth keeping.
